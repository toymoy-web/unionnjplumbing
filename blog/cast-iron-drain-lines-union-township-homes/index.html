Why So Many Union Township Homes Still Have Cast Iron Drain Lines

Published 2026-09-01 · Union Apex Plumbing

Walk through an older section of Union Township and there's a good chance the home you're standing near still drains through the same cast iron pipe it was built with, sometimes seventy or eighty years ago. Cast iron was the standard drain and vent material in residential construction through roughly the mid-20th century, before PVC became the industry default starting in the 1970s. In a dense, inner-ring suburb like Union, where a large share of the housing stock dates to the pre-war and mid-century construction boom, that means original cast iron drain lines are still doing their job in a real number of basements right now.

How Cast Iron Ages Differently Than PVC

Cast iron is genuinely durable, which is part of why so much of it is still in service decades later. But it ages in a specific way that PVC does not: the interior surface corrodes and scales over time, gradually narrowing the effective diameter of the pipe. A four-inch cast iron drain installed new might function closer to a two or three-inch opening after enough decades of mineral buildup and rust scale.

That narrowing is the direct cause of a pattern many Union Township homeowners recognize: a drain that used to handle normal household use without issue starts backing up more often, even though nothing about how the home is used has changed. The pipe itself has effectively gotten smaller.

Why Snaking Alone Doesn't Always Fix It

A cable snake is built to punch through a localized clog and restore flow. It does not remove the scale coating that caused the pipe to narrow in the first place. On a cast iron line with real interior buildup, that means a snake will often clear the immediate blockage, only for a similar clog to form again within a matter of weeks at roughly the same point in the line.

Hydro jetting addresses this differently. Instead of punching through the current clog, it uses a high-pressure water stream to scour the entire interior wall of the pipe, removing the buildup that caused the narrowing rather than just the symptom of it. For a line with a documented pattern of repeat clogs, jetting tends to be the fix that actually holds, even though it costs more upfront than a standard snaking call.

When Repair Makes Sense vs Replacement

Not every cast iron line needs to be replaced just because it's old. A line that's still structurally sound, with buildup as the main issue, is usually a good candidate for jetting rather than full replacement. The calculation changes when a camera inspection reveals actual cracks, offset joints from decades of ground settling, or sections that have corroded through in spots rather than just narrowed. At that point, a targeted spot repair or a longer-term replacement plan becomes the more honest recommendation.

Root intrusion is a related, separate issue that shows up often in the same older neighborhoods, since mature street trees common in established parts of Union Township will send roots toward any available moisture, including a hairline crack at a cast iron pipe joint. That combination, aging cast iron plus mature tree roots, is one of the more common reasons for a sewer line camera inspection to turn up something beyond a simple clog.

What This Means If You're Buying an Older Union Home

If you're purchasing a home in one of Union Township's older sections, it's worth asking directly whether the drain lines are original cast iron or have been updated, and if there's any history of repeat clogs or backups. A camera inspection before closing, while not universally standard, can catch a developing problem while it's still a targeted repair rather than a full line replacement after a backup causes water damage.

The Bottom Line

Cast iron drain lines aren't automatically a problem, and plenty of them have decades of good service left. But they age differently than modern PVC, and a pattern of repeat clogging in an older Union Township home is a real signal worth having checked with a camera rather than repeatedly calling for the same snaking service every few months.

What a Camera Inspection Actually Shows

A sewer or drain camera doesn't just confirm a clog exists, it shows exactly where the interior wall of the pipe is scaled, whether a joint has shifted from decades of ground settling, and whether root intrusion has entered at a specific point. That level of detail changes the conversation from a general sense that "the pipes are old" to a specific plan: this section needs jetting, this joint needs a spot repair, and this stretch is still in good structural shape despite its age. Homeowners are often surprised to learn that a cast iron line showing real narrowing on camera can still have plenty of useful life left once the interior buildup is properly cleared.

A Note on Vent Stacks

Cast iron shows up in vent stacks as well as drain lines, and a vent stack that's corroded enough to develop a small hole, often in an attic or where it passes through a roof, can cause slow drainage and gurgling throughout a home's plumbing system even though the actual clog isn't in a drain line at all. This is a less commonly recognized cause of drainage complaints in older Union Township homes, and it's part of why a full diagnostic visit sometimes turns up an answer different from what the original complaint suggested.

Cost Expectations for Cast Iron Work

Jetting an older cast iron line typically costs more than jetting PVC because of the extra care needed to avoid damaging already-thinned pipe walls, and a camera inspection beforehand is worth the added cost on any line with a documented repeat-clog history. A spot repair on a single failed section is almost always less expensive than treating the entire run as if it needs replacement, which is exactly why the diagnostic step matters before committing to a repair plan.
