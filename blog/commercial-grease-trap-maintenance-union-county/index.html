Grease Trap Maintenance for Union County Restaurants and Food Service

Published 2026-09-20 · Union Apex Plumbing

Grease trap maintenance sits in an unusual category for restaurant and food service operators in Union County: it's simultaneously an operational necessity, a code compliance requirement, and one of the more overlooked line items in day-to-day restaurant management until it causes a visible problem.

What a Grease Trap Actually Does

A grease trap intercepts fats, oils, and grease from kitchen wastewater before it reaches the municipal sewer system, allowing these substances to cool and separate from the water flow rather than traveling further down the line where they solidify and accumulate. Without this interception, grease from food service operations would rapidly narrow and eventually block sewer lines, both on the property and potentially further into the municipal system shared with other properties.

Why Regular Pumping Is Required, Not Optional

A grease trap has a finite capacity, and once it fills beyond a certain threshold, typically around 25 percent of its capacity by volume, its effectiveness at actually separating grease from wastewater drops significantly, allowing grease to pass through into the sewer line it's meant to protect. Local code in most Union County municipalities requires pumping on a defined schedule, often monthly to quarterly depending on the trap's size and the restaurant's volume, regardless of whether the trap appears full on casual inspection.

The Cost of Skipping Maintenance

A neglected grease trap doesn't just risk a compliance violation, it directly increases the likelihood of a sewer line backup on the property, a genuinely disruptive event for any food service operation that can mean an emergency closure and significant cleanup cost, well beyond what routine maintenance would have cost. Grease-related backups are also messier and more unpleasant to remediate than most other plumbing emergencies, given the nature of what's backing up.

Sizing and Placement Considerations

Grease trap sizing depends on the kitchen's actual output volume, and an undersized trap for a restaurant's real cooking volume will require more frequent pumping regardless of how diligent the maintenance schedule is. For properties undergoing renovation or a change in food service concept, reassessing whether the existing grease trap is still appropriately sized for the new operation is worth doing rather than assuming the prior tenant's equipment fits the new use.

Interior vs Exterior Grease Traps

Smaller interior grease traps, installed under a sink or in a compact kitchen footprint, generally need more frequent pumping given their limited capacity compared to larger exterior in-ground traps common at bigger food service operations. Both require the same fundamental maintenance discipline, just on different schedules matched to their respective capacities.

Working With a Property Manager or Landlord

In multi-tenant commercial properties, grease trap responsibility is sometimes shared or unclear between a restaurant tenant and property ownership, particularly for larger shared traps serving multiple food service tenants. Clarifying this responsibility explicitly, ideally in the lease terms rather than after a problem occurs, avoids a dispute at exactly the wrong moment, during an active backup or a compliance inspection.

What Compliance Documentation Looks Like

Most municipalities expect food service establishments to maintain records of grease trap pumping, sometimes requested during health inspections or in response to a reported issue. Keeping straightforward, dated service records isn't just good practice, it's often the actual documentation a municipal inspector will ask to see.

The Practical Bottom Line

Grease trap maintenance is one of the more predictable, schedulable maintenance items a food service property has, and treating it as a fixed operational cost rather than a deferrable expense avoids the significantly larger cost and disruption of an actual backup. A consistent pumping schedule matched to your specific kitchen's volume is the straightforward, unglamorous solution to what could otherwise become a genuinely disruptive problem.

New Restaurant Openings

For a new restaurant opening in a previously non-food-service space, grease trap installation is often part of the buildout process, and getting the sizing right from the start avoids the more disruptive scenario of retrofitting an undersized trap after operations have already begun. We work with restaurant owners and their contractors during buildout to confirm grease trap sizing matches the planned kitchen's actual projected output rather than a generic standard.

Signs Your Current Schedule Isn't Frequent Enough

Slow-draining kitchen sinks, a persistent grease odor near the trap location, or a sewer backup traced to grease buildup are all signs that a current pumping schedule may not match actual kitchen output, even if it technically meets the minimum code-required frequency. Kitchen volume can increase over time as a restaurant's business grows, and a pumping schedule set years ago at a lower volume may simply no longer be adequate. Reassessing frequency periodically, rather than treating the original schedule as permanent, keeps the trap functioning as intended as your business changes.

Choosing a Service Provider You Can Rely On

Grease trap pumping requires proper disposal of the collected waste through a licensed hauler, and a restaurant owner is ultimately responsible for confirming their service provider handles this correctly rather than simply assuming it. Asking for disposal documentation as part of routine service isn't an unusual request, it's a reasonable part of maintaining your own compliance record in case a municipal inspector ever asks for it. A provider who can't or won't produce this documentation is worth reconsidering regardless of price. Union County health inspectors do periodically ask for this specific paperwork, and having it ready is a meaningfully smoother experience than scrambling to request it after the fact. Building this into a standing vendor relationship, rather than shopping for the cheapest one-off pump each time, tends to produce more consistent record-keeping over the life of the business and fewer surprises at inspection time, which matters more than the modest price difference between providers over a full year.
