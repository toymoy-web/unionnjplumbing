A Sump Pump Maintenance Checklist Before Union County's Storm Season

Published 2026-09-12 · Union Apex Plumbing

A sump pump that's never been checked between storms is a real gamble, since the first sign of a problem is often the exact moment it's needed most during a heavy rain event. A short seasonal maintenance routine, done before Union County's heaviest rain periods in late summer and fall, catches most developing issues while there's still time to address them.

Step One: Test the Float Switch

Pour a bucket of water into the sump pit until the float switch triggers the pump. It should activate promptly, run smoothly, and shut off once the water level drops back down. A delayed activation, uneven cycling, or a pump that runs but doesn't seem to actually lower the water level all point to a problem worth investigating before, not during, the next storm.

Step Two: Check the Discharge Line

Follow the discharge line outside and confirm it's directing water well away from the foundation, not just a few feet from the house where it can simply seep back toward the basement. In winter, check that the outdoor discharge point isn't at risk of freezing shut, which would leave the pump running with nowhere for the water to go.

Step Three: Inspect the Check Valve

The check valve prevents water in the discharge line from flowing back into the pit once the pump shuts off. A failed or missing check valve means the pump has to immediately re-pump the same water that just drained back down, wearing it out faster and reducing effective capacity during a heavy rain event when every cycle matters.

Step Four: Clean the Pit and Inlet Screen

Debris accumulating in the sump pit or clogging the pump's inlet screen reduces efficiency and can eventually cause the pump to jam entirely. A quick visual check and cleaning, especially if the pit hasn't been inspected in over a year, is a simple step with real payoff.

Step Five: Confirm Battery Backup Function

If your system includes a battery backup, and given Union County's real storm-related power outage risk it's worth having one, check that the battery holds a charge and that the backup pump activates correctly when primary power is simulated as off. A backup battery that's been silently dying for months provides no actual protection exactly when it's needed.

Step Six: Note the Pump's Age

Most residential sump pumps have a service life of roughly 7 to 10 years under regular use. If yours is approaching or past that range, even if it currently passes every test above, proactive replacement ahead of storm season is a reasonable decision rather than waiting for an in-season failure.

When to Call a Professional Instead of DIY-Checking

If any of these steps reveal a problem, an unusual noise, a pump that won't activate, a battery that won't hold charge, or a check valve that's clearly failed, that's the point to call rather than attempt a repair without the right parts or diagnostic equipment on hand. Catching this in a routine pre-storm check, rather than during an active flood, is exactly the point of doing this maintenance proactively.

Why This Matters More in This Part of New Jersey

Union County's density and the Rahway and Elizabeth River watersheds running through the region mean real basement flood risk during heavy Mid-Atlantic storms, a risk that's compounded when storms also knock out power at the same time a sump pump is working hardest. A working, tested system going into storm season is genuinely one of the more effective preventive investments available to homeowners in this specific area.

Keeping a Simple Maintenance Log

Noting the date of each test, any issues found, and when the pump was last replaced or serviced takes only a moment but creates a real record over time, useful both for tracking the pump's age accurately and for insurance purposes if a flood event does occur despite reasonable preventive maintenance. A simple note on your phone or a small notebook kept near the utility area is sufficient; the value is in having the record at all, not in any particular format.

What a Secondary Pump Adds

For basements with a documented flood history or finished space where water damage cost is especially high, a secondary backup pump, either battery-powered or water-pressure-powered as a redundant system alongside the primary electric pump, provides an additional layer of protection if the primary pump itself fails mechanically rather than just losing power. This is a more significant investment than a standard battery backup alone, worth discussing specifically for higher-risk properties rather than as a universal recommendation for every home in our service area.

A Realistic Annual Schedule

A reasonable annual routine looks like this: a full function test each spring before the heaviest rain season begins, a follow-up check in early fall, and a battery replacement or professional service call every few years depending on the backup system's specific maintenance recommendations. This isn't an elaborate undertaking, but it's meaningfully more protection than the common alternative of never checking the system at all until the moment a storm actually arrives. Two brief checks a year is a small commitment against the real cost of an unexpected basement flood.

What to Do Immediately After a Confirmed Flood

If a sump pump failure has already resulted in water entering your basement despite these precautions, extracting the water quickly and beginning structural drying within the first day matters more than almost any other factor in limiting total damage and mold risk. Address the immediate flooding first, then circle back to diagnosing why the pump failed once the water itself is under control, rather than trying to solve both problems simultaneously under pressure.
