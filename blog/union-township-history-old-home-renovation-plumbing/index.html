Renovating an Old Union Township Home: What the Plumbing Behind the Walls Might Tell You

Published 2026-09-10 · Union Apex Plumbing

Union Township traces its settlement back to 1749, when the area was known as Connecticut Farms, and its incorporation as a separate municipality followed in 1808. The township was also the site of the Battle of Connecticut Farms on June 7, 1780, part of the same British campaign that included the Battle of Springfield days later, a genuine piece of Revolutionary War history that predates almost everything else discussed in a typical home renovation conversation. That long history means a real share of the township's housing stock spans construction eras from the early 1900s through the mid-century boom, and renovating any of it means confronting whatever plumbing decisions were made decades before current code and materials existed.

What Opening a Wall Actually Reveals

The most common surprise in an older Union Township renovation is discovering that a previous remodel updated visible fixtures without touching the plumbing rough-in behind them. A kitchen that got a cosmetic update in the 1990s might still be running on drain and supply lines original to the home's construction decades earlier, meaning the visible fixtures look current while the actual infrastructure doing the work is far older than a homeowner might assume.

Cast Iron and Galvanized Pipe

Homes from the pre-war and mid-century construction era commonly used cast iron for drain and vent lines and galvanized steel for supply lines, both standard materials for their time but both prone to age-related issues: cast iron narrows internally from corrosion and scale, while galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside in a way that eventually restricts flow and can lead to leaks at threaded joints. Finding either during a renovation is common, not alarming, but it's worth addressing while the wall is already open rather than closing everything back up around materials nearing the end of their practical service life.

Lead Service Lines: A Real Possibility in Older Homes

Some of the oldest housing stock in Union Township and neighboring older Essex and Union County municipalities may still have lead water service lines, a material that was standard practice before being phased out for health reasons. If your home's service line material has never been confirmed, a renovation project is a natural point to have this checked, since replacement is a meaningfully more important priority than most other opportunistic plumbing updates a renovation might otherwise support.

Sewer Laterals and Mature Trees

Union Township's established neighborhoods often have mature street trees planted decades ago, and their root systems have had ample time to find their way into any hairline crack or joint in an aging clay or cast iron sewer lateral. A renovation project, especially one involving foundation or basement work, is a reasonable time to have the sewer lateral camera-inspected if it hasn't been checked recently, since problems here are easier and less expensive to address proactively than after a backup during the middle of a larger renovation.

Updating to Current Code

Any plumbing work performed as part of a renovation needs to meet current New Jersey plumbing code, even in a home where the surrounding, untouched plumbing predates that code. This means new work, whether a repiped section, a relocated fixture, or a new gas line, gets built correctly even if it now coexists temporarily with older, non-code sections elsewhere in the home.

A Practical Approach for Union Township Renovators

Before finalizing a renovation scope, having a plumber assess what's actually running behind the walls, not just what the renovation plan explicitly calls for, helps avoid the common situation of closing up a wall around plumbing that would have been far easier to address while already exposed. Given how much of Union Township's housing carries genuine multi-generational history, that assessment is less about assuming the worst and more about making an informed decision with real information rather than a guess.

Budgeting for the Unknown

A realistic renovation budget for an older Union Township home should include a contingency for plumbing discoveries beyond the original scope, since opening walls in a home with this much construction history genuinely does turn up surprises at a higher rate than in newer housing. This isn't a reason to avoid renovating an older home, but it is a reason to set expectations honestly with a contractor and plumber upfront rather than treating every discovered issue as an unwelcome surprise mid-project.

Preserving What's Worth Preserving

Not every piece of original plumbing infrastructure needs to be replaced simply because it's old. Some cast iron drain sections in genuinely good structural condition, confirmed by camera inspection, still have real useful life left, and replacing them purely because a wall happens to be open isn't always the most cost-effective choice. We aim to give a clear, camera-verified read on what's actually worth replacing during a renovation versus what can reasonably stay in service, rather than defaulting to a blanket recommendation either way.

Coordinating With a General Contractor

On a larger renovation, plumbing work needs to be sequenced correctly with framing, drywall, and finishing trades, and a plumber who's used to coordinating within that larger project timeline, rather than treating plumbing as an isolated task, helps the overall renovation stay on schedule. Discussing the plumbing scope directly with your general contractor early in planning, rather than as an afterthought once walls are already open, tends to produce a smoother project overall and avoids costly rework when trades aren't sequenced correctly, particularly around inspection sign-offs that depend on plumbing being complete before drywall closes the wall back up for good. Bringing a plumber into the planning conversation at the same point you bring in your general contractor, rather than after demolition has already started, is the single easiest way to avoid this kind of costly resequencing on an older Union Township home.
