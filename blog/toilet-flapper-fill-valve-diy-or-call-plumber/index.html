Running Toilet? Flapper vs Fill Valve, and When to Call a Plumber

Published 2026-09-14 · Union Apex Plumbing

A running toilet is one of the most common plumbing complaints, and it almost always comes down to one of two components: the flapper or the fill valve. Understanding which one is causing the problem, and whether it's a reasonable DIY fix or worth calling a plumber, saves both time and unnecessary water waste while you figure it out.

How to Tell Which Component Is the Problem

Remove the toilet tank lid and watch the water level after a flush cycle completes. If water is continuously trickling into the overflow tube, the fill valve isn't shutting off properly. If the water level looks normal and stable but you can still hear or see water slowly leaving the tank, the flapper isn't sealing the drain opening completely, letting tank water slowly leak into the bowl.

The Flapper: Usually the Simpler Fix

The flapper is the rubber or silicone seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when you flush and should drop back down to seal the tank's drain opening. Over time, flappers degrade, warp slightly, or accumulate mineral deposits that prevent a complete seal. A flapper replacement is genuinely one of the more DIY-accessible plumbing repairs, involving disconnecting the old flapper from its mounting and connecting a properly sized replacement, available at any hardware store for a modest cost.

The Fill Valve: Slightly More Involved

The fill valve controls water refilling the tank after a flush and should shut off once the water reaches the correct level, set by an adjustable float. A fill valve that won't shut off completely, or that's been improperly adjusted so the water level sits too high and constantly drains into the overflow tube, causes continuous running. Fill valve replacement is a bit more involved than a flapper swap, since it requires shutting off the water supply to the toilet and disconnecting the supply line, but it's still within reach for a comfortable DIY homeowner.

When It's Worth Just Calling a Plumber

If you've replaced the flapper or fill valve and the toilet is still running, the problem may be something less obvious, a crack in the tank itself, a problem with the flush valve seat that the flapper seals against, or an issue with the toilet's specific design that a generic replacement part doesn't properly address. At that point, continuing to guess and replace parts one at a time usually costs more in time and materials than having it properly diagnosed.

Similarly, if you're not comfortable shutting off water supply valves, especially an older valve that hasn't been turned in years and may be difficult to close fully, that's a reasonable point to call rather than risk a bigger problem from a valve that won't close as expected.

The Cost of Ignoring It

A running toilet wastes real water continuously, and depending on the severity, this adds up to a meaningful amount on a water bill over just a few months. Beyond the direct cost, a fill valve that's failing can eventually fail more seriously, so a toilet that's been running for weeks is worth addressing rather than treating as background noise.

A Note on Multiple Toilets Failing Around the Same Time

If several toilets in an older Union Township home installed around the same original construction date start having similar issues within a short window of each other, that's often simply the shared age of the original internal components reaching end of life together, rather than a coincidence. In that situation, addressing all of them in one visit is usually more efficient than treating each as a separate emergency as it happens.

A Quick Test Before You Buy Replacement Parts

Before purchasing a flapper or fill valve replacement, a simple food coloring test can confirm which component is actually the issue if the running pattern isn't obvious from watching the tank alone. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank water, wait about fifteen minutes without flushing, then check the bowl. If color has appeared in the bowl, the flapper isn't sealing properly. If the bowl remains clear but the tank continues quietly refilling, the fill valve is the more likely culprit. This five-minute test avoids buying and installing the wrong part on a guess.

Standard Parts Fit Most Toilets

The good news for anyone attempting a DIY fix is that flappers and fill valves are largely standardized across toilet brands and models manufactured over the past few decades, with universal replacement kits available at any hardware store that fit the vast majority of residential toilets. Older, less common toilet models occasionally use a proprietary design that needs a specific matching part, which is usually the point where a generic hardware store fix doesn't quite work and professional diagnosis becomes the more efficient path.

A Note on Water Bills as a Diagnostic Tool

If you're unsure whether a toilet has actually been running or it's an intermittent issue you've only caught occasionally, comparing recent water bills against the same period a year earlier can confirm whether there's a genuine, ongoing waste pattern worth tracking down. A meaningful, unexplained increase supports the running-toilet theory even on days you haven't personally heard or seen it happening. This same comparison is useful more broadly any time a leak is suspected but hasn't been directly observed.

A Reasonable Rule of Thumb for This Repair Category

As a general guide, if you can identify the specific failed part with reasonable confidence and feel comfortable shutting off the relevant water supply safely, a DIY attempt with a standard replacement part is a reasonable first step. If either of those conditions isn't true, or a first attempt doesn't resolve the issue, that's the natural point to call rather than keep guessing at parts one at a time.
